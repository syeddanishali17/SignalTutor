**Picture it.** Push on a thin membrane and it bends. Its surface stretches in some places and is squeezed in others — by a tiny fraction.

**What it is.** Strain is the change of length as a fraction of the length:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} \qquad (\text{no unit; } +\text{ stretched},\ -\text{ compressed})$$

Real strains are tiny, so they are quoted in **microstrain**: $1\,\mu\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$. A pressure-sensor membrane sees a few hundred µε.

The force per area that causes it is the **stress** $\sigma$ (unit Pa). For small stretches they are proportional, $\sigma = \varepsilon\cdot E$, where **Young's modulus** $E$ measures stiffness: steel ≈ 200 GPa, silicon ≈ 130–190 GPa depending on the crystal direction.

**A bent membrane, clamped at its rim.** On the surface that the pressure pushes on, the region near the **rim stretches** and the region near the **centre is squeezed** (like the top and bottom faces of a bent ruler, but with the curvature reversing between rim and centre). Gauges placed in both regions see strains of opposite sign — the trick behind the full bridge.

**Why sensors care.** A strained resistor changes its resistance. That is the gauge factor — next.
