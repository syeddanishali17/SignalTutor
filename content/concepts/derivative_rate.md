Read $\dfrac{dQ}{dt}$ as a fraction of two **tiny changes**:

$$\frac{dQ}{dt} \approx \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \frac{\text{change in charge}}{\text{time it took}}$$

* numerator: how much $Q$ changed; denominator: in how much time.
* On a graph of $Q$ against $t$ it is the **slope**.

**Key idea for sensors:** a quantity can be *large* and still have *zero* rate of change. A capacitor holding a big, constant charge has $dQ/dt = 0$: **no current flows**. Current appears only while the charge *changes*.

$$I = \frac{dQ}{dt} \qquad (\text{amperes} = \text{coulombs per second})$$

Two useful slopes: the slope of $\sin(\omega t)$ is $\omega\cos(\omega t)$, and the slope of $\cos(\omega t)$ is $-\omega\sin(\omega t)$ — faster wiggles (bigger $\omega$) mean steeper slopes.
